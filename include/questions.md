# Questions You Should Ask in an Interview

Just in case it is not obvious: during an interview, they are not only evaluating you, you are also evaluating the
potential workplace. Coming in, solving tasks, and going home to wait for a call is not a great idea. To understand the
company and working conditions, you should ask questions. For example:

## The job overall

- What kind of projects would I work on first?
- Can you show me examples of projects I will work on?
- What skills and experience do you expect from an ideal candidate?
- Is this a new role in the company?

### **How old is the project?**

```markdown
The older the project, the higher the chance it is legacy code with frequent team turnover.

If the project is under five years old, it is probably fine.

If the system is 15+ years old, it may be a questionable experience.
If it is somewhere in between, it is hard to tell.
```

### **How "bloody" is your enterprise on a scale from 1 to 10?**

```markdown
How flexible is the schedule? Is remote work possible, for example during illness?

Does a manager hover and ask every 10 minutes when it will be done, or do they set a task and let you work?

Do you have to report daily how many hours went into each task?

Do developers wear shirts and ties, and so on?
```

### **How stressful is the work?**

```markdown
Are there constant production incidents, regular overtime, unrealistic deadlines, late-night support calls, a
constantly yelling boss, and so on? Most people do not enjoy that.
```

### **Which development methodology does the team use? Scrum, Waterfall, XP, or what?**

```markdown
The right answer is Agile: Scrum or Kanban.
A completely wrong answer is Waterfall or "I don't know".
```

### **Tell me more about your workflow. Who requests new features, who writes specs, how is testing done, which bug
tracker and version control systems are used?**

```markdown
A good answer sounds like this:
Managers request features, analysts write specs, testing happens in several stages (functional, load, alpha, beta)
by a testing team, and we use Jira and Git.
In some companies, managers write specs directly in Jira, which is fine.
The absence of version control or a testing team is usually a red flag.
```

### **Why are you hiring?**

```markdown
A good answer: the business is growing and there is a lot of work.
A bad answer: the whole development team quit last week.
```

### **What exactly will I be doing? Also, which compilers, operating systems, and DBMS versions are used?**

```markdown
A proper answer is: "You will write the part that processes quotes in Erlang, we use Erlang R16 and LevelDB, and it
runs on CentOS."

An answer like: "We are building a high-load startup, but we cannot tell you more because the client required an NDA"
should make you cautious.

An answer like: "We need a professional CSS developer with knowledge of JavaScript, Ruby, NoSQL, and Django" is very,
very wrong.
```

## Learning and professional development

- What opportunities for career growth and professional development exist?

## Performance evaluation

- Tell me about the employee performance evaluation process. How often does it happen?
- What metrics will be used to evaluate my work?
- What are the most important things you expect me to achieve in the first 30, 60, and 90 days?
- What results are expected from this role within the first 12 months?

## The interviewer

- How long have you been with the company?
- Why did you join this company?
- What do you like most about working here?

## The company overall

- How do you see the company in the next few years?
- What can you tell me about your new products and/or growth plans?
- What are the company's main goals right now, and how is it working toward them?

### **Can you tell me about your benefits package?**

```markdown
If there is no benefits package and only salary and 28 days of vacation, that is not great.

If there are free coffee and snacks, free lunches, health insurance, money for a new laptop, English classes at the
office, paid conference attendance, and other perks, that is a big plus.
```

## Team

- Can you tell me about the team I will work with?
- Who will I work most closely with?
- What career prospects exist in this department?

### **What is the team size?**

```markdown
If the team is up to five or six developers, that is OK.

If it is larger, it is a reason to think. Maybe management is trying to hit a deadline by hiring more people.
Or maybe bugs are coming in so fast that the team only has time to fix them.
```

### **Can I see where your developers work?**

```markdown
"No" is a bad answer. It is bad if developers work in a large, stuffy open space with accounting and sales.
It is bad if developers sit in narrow cubicles. If developers sit in an old shed next to a pile of noisy one-unit
servers, it is very, very bad.

It is good if developers work separately from other colleagues in a small, quiet, air-conditioned room for around
seven people, with wide desks and comfortable chairs, and with open space behind them.
```

## Culture

- What is the company culture in general and the team culture in particular?
- How would you describe the working environment: team-based or mostly individual?
- Do employees/team members meet outside the office?
- Can you tell me about the last team event?

## Next steps

- How well do you think my skills match this role?
- What are the next steps in the hiring process?
- Is there anything else you need from me?
- Can I answer any other questions you have?

### **How long will you need to make a decision?**

```markdown
Just to avoid misunderstandings. Some places call the next day, some interview many candidates for months and choose a
few favorites, and some are not actually hiring at all.

You can ask about code quality and turnover, but people will rarely answer honestly.

You can ask about project load (requests per second, number of users, and so on), but in my experience, it is not a
major factor.

Do not ask about salary at this point. The interviewer will bring it up. By the way, some of my thoughts on job search,
including how to answer salary expectation questions, can be found in an old note.

Also note: you should not only listen to answers, but watch how the person behaves while answering.
A lot of communication is non-verbal.

It matters who interviews you. It is good if developers do it, less good if it is the CTO, bad if it is HR. And if you
are interviewed by a recruiting agency, then I have no idea what you are thinking.
```

### **Tell me about salary. I personally think a fully declared salary is a plus.**

```markdown
Find out if there are bonuses. I used to think bonuses were a scam, but others may disagree.

Find out how often salaries are reviewed. Once a year is good, never is bad.

Ask if there is a transparent salary growth scheme. It is good if there is one. For example, some companies increase
pay by a fixed amount per supported project, while others increase by a percentage each year.

Be aware that asking about salary for too long can make a negative impression.
```
